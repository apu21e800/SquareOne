There is a junction on Vancouver Island that used to need three signs. A "Yield" sign for the inbound traffic. A "Pedestrian Crossing" sign for the foot traffic. A "Watch for Oncoming" warning for everyone else. Three signs, and drivers still misread the geometry.

Then we installed a brick.

Or, more accurately, we installed the appearance of brick — stamped into asphalt with a StreetPrint imprinting system, sealed in a deep terracotta colour, finished with a clean white border that rings the entire centre island. The transformation was done in a single weekend, no demolition, no road rebuild. The signs are still there. But the drivers don't need them anymore.

## What happened

The colour and texture do something that signs cannot. They tell drivers, before language has time to register, that *the road has changed*. Asphalt becomes brick. Speed becomes attention. The visual cue is processed in the part of the brain that handles environment, not the part that handles instruction. By the time a driver has decided to slow down, they have already done it.

Traffic engineers have a term for this — *self-explaining roads*. Roads designed so the appropriate behaviour is obvious from the geometry and the surface alone. Brick roundabouts are perhaps the cleanest example. The texture announces "this is a yield zone" without anyone having to read a single word.

## What we used

The roundabout surface is StreetPrint — a stamped-asphalt imprinting system. The asphalt is laid hot, the imprint mats are pressed in while it's still pliable, and the colour is applied as a heat-cured surface coating. The result is a textured surface that performs as well as plain asphalt under a snowplow blade and lasts as long as the road around it.

For the centre island, we worked with the engineering drawings to keep all crossing geometry MUTCD-compliant. The decorative treatment doesn't replace any safety markings — it *amplifies* them. The white perimeter you see in the photograph is high-friction, high-visibility coating, set against the deep red of the roundabout body so drivers register the boundary at a glance.

## What it cost

A traditional roundabout rebuild — full curb work, drainage, paver installation — is a six-figure project that closes the road for weeks. Our installation reused the existing geometry and asphalt; the entire treatment was applied to the existing road in a long weekend. The municipality booked it on a Friday afternoon and reopened on Monday morning.

For the cost of the signage budget over the next five years, the road got something signs cannot give it: a surface that says, on its own, *slow down here*.

## Why this matters for BC

Vancouver Island has a particular geography problem. Most municipalities are coastal, mountain-bordered, with corridors that funnel traffic through small junctions. The traditional fix is more signs, more lights, more lane paint. The colonial road-marking vocabulary.

Decorative pavement gives a different vocabulary. It lets the road *itself* communicate. And in a province where the views are this good, anything that quiets the visual noise of road infrastructure tends to be welcomed.

This roundabout was approved on a Tuesday. We were on site that Friday. By Monday morning, the corridor was different — and nobody had to read a thing.
