Commercial Drive, Vancouver. The stretch of the Drive that Vancouverites still call Little Italy now carries crosswalks rendered in green, white, and red — the colours of the Italian flag, installed into the intersections themselves. We installed the work in TrafficPatterns preformed thermoplastic on behalf of the City of Vancouver, in coordination with the Commercial Drive Business Society.

This is a case study on neighbourhood-branded pavement art and why the product choice matters when the pattern is supposed to read as identity, not as paint.

## Why Neighbourhood Branding on the Ground

Vancouver has been working for years on what urbanists call "place-identity infrastructure" — the small, repeated civic gestures that tell residents and visitors what neighbourhood they are standing in. Signage is one lever. Street furniture is another. Pavement art is a third, and it is the one that most directly shapes how a neighbourhood reads from a pedestrian's eye level.

For Little Italy, the flag-colour crosswalks reinforce the identity that the Drive has carried for three generations. Vancouver's Italian community established itself along Commercial Drive in the postwar decades, and while the demographics of the Drive have broadened, the neighbourhood's Italian roots remain part of its public identity. Rendering that identity into the crosswalks is a gesture that meets the community where it has always been.

## TrafficPatterns for a Crosswalk Brand Install

Crosswalk branding has a technical problem painted markings can't solve. The crosswalk surface takes direct vehicular loading from turning and through traffic. It takes winter salt and abrasion. It takes rain, freeze, and UV across a four-season cycle. A painted tricolour crosswalk on Commercial Drive would read beautifully on the day it was installed and would be visibly degraded within eighteen months.

TrafficPatterns solves that problem through its chemistry. The preformed thermoplastic sheets are torch-applied to the asphalt — the material fuses into the substrate rather than sitting on top of it. Colour runs through the full depth of the sheet, which means mechanical abrasion from tire contact exposes more pigmented material rather than wearing the colour off.

Expected service life on the Commercial Drive installation is in the seven-to-ten-year window at full colour saturation, with the pattern holding recognizable through the back half of that service window even as the material softens.

## Pattern Layout in an Active Corridor

Commercial Drive does not close for pavement work. The Drive is one of Vancouver's busiest commercial corridors, with retail operating on both sides of the street and pedestrian traffic heavy through the daytime and evening hours.

We scheduled the install in coordinated overnight blocks, working intersection by intersection. Each intersection's install was sequenced so the crosswalk could be closed, prepped, marked, installed, and reopened inside the overnight window — no half-finished crosswalks left for morning traffic.

Substrate prep was the longest portion of each overnight block. The asphalt at a downtown intersection accumulates oil deposits, gum, and surface film that have to be cleared before a thermoplastic install will bond cleanly. We use mechanical and vapor-blast prep depending on the substrate condition. At each Commercial Drive intersection, we assessed the asphalt before the install team arrived with the thermoplastic sheets.

## The Tricolour Layout

The flag-colour crosswalks use the standard vertical tricolour — green, white, red — oriented so a pedestrian crossing the Drive walks through the stripes in sequence. The proportions are set to meet City of Vancouver crosswalk-visibility standards while preserving the recognizability of the flag pattern.

That proportional balance is the kind of decision that matters more than it seems. A tricolour pattern that reads as flag but fails the visibility standard would have been rejected by the city's engineering review. A tricolour pattern that meets the engineering standard but reads as stripes rather than flag would have failed the placemaking brief. The final layout threads both.

## What Commercial Drive Reads Like Now

A pedestrian crossing Commercial Drive at one of the branded intersections walks across a surface that says, without any accompanying signage, that this is Little Italy. A driver approaching the intersection registers the colour blocks from half a block out, which reinforces the crosswalk's role as a safety marking as well as a placemaking gesture.

The Business Society reported stronger foot-traffic response in the months following the install, particularly around the summer festival calendar that draws visitors to the Drive. Whether that response is directly attributable to the crosswalks or to coincident programming is the kind of question a civic-economics study would have to answer, but the anecdotal evidence suggests the branded crosswalks are doing their placemaking work.
