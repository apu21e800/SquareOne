Last year we finished a full school-zone markings package at South Langford Elementary on Vancouver Island. Crosswalks, school-logo pavement graphics, speed-limit legends, and the accessible pathway lines running from the front gate to the drop-off loop.

It's the kind of project that doesn't photograph like a transit corridor or a stamped asphalt driveway. But when it's installed right, it changes how drivers behave in that zone — and for parents standing on the sidewalk in the morning, that's the measurable outcome.

## The Product: Preformed Thermoplastic

All the coloured markings in a school zone — the yellow crosswalk bars, the blue accessible symbols, the school-crossing shields, the patterned pavement logos — come from a product family called preformed thermoplastic.

TrafficPatterns is one of the systems we install most often. It comes pre-cut in sheets. We torch-heat the surface and the sheet simultaneously, and the plastic fuses into the asphalt as it cools. Unlike paint, it doesn't sit on top of the road. It becomes part of the road.

The reason this matters at a school: paint survives maybe two winters before it loses reflectivity. Preformed thermoplastic holds its colour and its retro-reflective glass beads for five to seven years, in real BC conditions. At a school, which handles hundreds of drop-off and pick-up cycles per week, that durability translates directly into whether a driver can still read the crosswalk at 7:30am in December rain.

## What Actually Slows Drivers Down

There's a specific combination that works in school zones:

A textured, high-contrast crosswalk bar — the yellow blocks you see laid in a ladder pattern — registers from much further out than a painted line. A driver's eye catches the pattern break before the sign does.

A pavement-logo decal — the school crest or a large school-zone shield — right before the zone. It's not just branding. It's a visual interruption. Drivers slow because the surface suddenly has information on it.

A painted speed-limit legend, large format, placed in-lane. Reinforces the sign. A driver who somehow missed the posted sign still reads the number on the ground.

Each of these on its own is modestly effective. Installed as a sequence, they compound.

## Why the Install Has to Be Clean

Preformed thermoplastic is an unforgiving product at install. If the asphalt underneath is damp — and in BC, especially in the shoulder seasons, it's almost always damp somewhere — the fusion doesn't happen correctly and the marking lifts within a season.

We schedule school-zone work in the dry window and we check surface moisture with a meter before any torch goes on a sheet. Not because the specification requires it, but because the alternative is a crosswalk that starts peeling at its edges by next September, with a school district asking what happened.

The actual install is also noisy, smoky, and involves open flame — so it happens outside school hours. Most of our school-zone work runs evenings or weekends, with full site cleanup before the first staff arrive.

## Specifying for a District

If you're in a BC school district looking at a summer markings refresh — or you're working with an engineering firm scoping a new school site — the decisions that matter are the material selection, the reflective-bead specification, and the crew's experience with thermoplastic at scale.

Paint is cheaper per square metre. It is not cheaper over seven years.
